ZeroFACT: Zero-Shot Force-Aware Contact Transfer for Contact-Rich Dexterous Manipulation

Force-aware contact targets, tracked in real time.

ZeroFACT studies whether contact-rich dexterous skills can be transferred zero-shot from human demonstrations by treating active contact and contact force as transferable interaction intent.

Retargeting can recover the geometry of contact: which fingers touch the object, where contacts occur, and how the hand moves through the task. However, geometry alone does not determine the physical interaction. We therefore pair each retargeted kinematic target with a force target, and use a real-time robot-side controller to track the desired contact condition during execution.

Where does force enter the pipeline?

We propose to compare three ways for force to enter the pipeline: as an offline label transformation, as a feedforward prediction target, or as a closed-loop control objective.

Structure 3 instead closes the loop with a real-time refinement controller

Designing this controller is the central challenge of the proposal. It has to track force at a rate the policy cannot reach, act only once contact is established, and stay stable under tactile noise. Everything else in the pipeline exists to give it a target worth tracking.

where q is the robot kinematic state, F is the measured fingertip contact force, k is a fixed spring coefficient, and N is the prediction horizon on the controller clock

Hypothesis. Closed-loop force tracking is necessary for reliable zero-shot transfer of contact-rich dexterous skills. Keeping force as an explicit target, and tracking it with a real-time controller, should produce policies that are compatible with physical interaction rather than only with kinematic imitation.

From human demonstration to robot labels

Hand pose provides the retargeted robot kinematic state, while glove tactile readings provide the active contact set and, after calibration, the corresponding contact-force targets.

Evaluation

Metrics.

  1. Policy success rate for learned execution.
  2. Replay force error for force tracking.
  3. Replay success rate for validating the demonstration-to-controller pipeline.

Proposed Contributions

  1. A controlled study of where force enters the imitation learning stack. We compare several force-aware designs and use policy success, force tracking, and replay reliability to reveal how different force designs behave, where they help, and where they fail.
  2. A reusable force-refinement layer. We design a real-time middle layer that refines position and force targets within the robot’s capability, and requests replanning when a target is unreachable.
  3. A complete robot-learning system. We build the full stack: glove calibration, human-to-robot retargeting, force-aware control, and policy learning.

Milestones

  1. Hardware replay. Replay one recorded contact-rich trajectory on the real robot using both kinematic and force targets. This validates the full data path from glove pressure to calibrated force, from human hand pose to robot contact targets, and from targets to executable controller commands.
  2. Policy-in-the-loop execution. Integrate a learned policy with the real-time controller on a simple contact-rich task. The goal is to demonstrate stable closed-loop execution before scaling to broader task families.
  3. Full structure comparison. Implement all three policy–controller structures and evaluate them across the proposed task families. This tests whether explicit closed-loop force tracking improves zero-shot contact-rich transfer.

Bonus / Extensions

Reinforcement learning. Reconstruct the controller stack in simulation and learn the refinement layer instead of hand-designing it. The main challenge is simulating force or tactile feedback with sufficient fidelity for transfer.

Bimanual contact-rich manipulation. The initial study focuses on one hand. Extending to two hands introduces coupled contacts, load sharing, and coordination constraints, making it a distinct research problem rather than a direct scale-up.

Latent tactile targets. Force is the simplest explicit contact target. A richer tactile latent could represent contact state more generally, but unlike force, it does not provide an obvious direction for local control. A learned RL refinement layer could optimize such latent targets directly without requiring an explicit tactile Jacobian.

Deployment without tactile sensors. Many robot hands do not include tactile sensing. A compliant controller may provide a proprioceptive contact proxy: persistent error between commanded and measured joint states indicates external resistance. If this proxy can estimate contact force reliably, force-aware manipulation could transfer to hands without tactile sensors.

Comparison with related work

Swipe the table sideways to see every column.

Contact-Grounded Policy [7] corresponds to structure 2, and learns force-aware behavior from teleoperated robot demonstrations. ReForce [1] is the closest to structure 3: a residual force tracker, learned in simulation, corrects the retargeted action in closed loop from measured fingertip force. Its policy, however, sees neither measured force nor an object-centric point cloud, so its tasks run blind.

In contrast, this proposal studies force-aware transfer from human demonstrations across all three structures, with what the policy observes — measured force, object-centric point clouds — as part of what is varied.
