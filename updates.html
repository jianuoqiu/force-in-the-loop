Week 4

Sep 21 – Sep 27, 2026

Week 3: 0 of 80 closed-loop successes on fresh starts. This week: in-domain evaluation. Every rollout starts from the bit-identical initial state of one of the policy's own training episodes, so the recording is a frame-by-frame reference. Same controller, physics and success test throughout; only the source of the reference chunk changes. A dataset section then describes the small-range set built for the next round, and Result 5 tests a standing objection to the controller itself: that generating fingertip force through a position servo is the wrong channel for a closed force loop.

Result 3In-domain closed-loop evaluation: a four-rung ladder

Four ways of producing the reference the controller tracks, from a pure replay to the real closed loop. Each rung isolates one thing: whether the evaluation stack works, whether a measured-state reference is executable at all, whether the policy forecasts well on the training distribution, and whether it can drive itself.

1 · Expert commands, exact replay. The recorded motor targets, held per frame, no force law. If this fails the infrastructure is broken.

2 · Perfect forecast. No network: the recording's own future measured states, smoothed forces and contacts are served as the chunk, i.e. exactly what the policy is trained to output, with zero error. The ceiling of this reference type plus the controller.

3 · Policy on recorded observations. The real checkpoint, but at every state it receives the recording's observation (image or cloud, joints, velocities, forces, contacts). Its forecasts are executed in the live simulator; it never sees the consequences. Teacher forcing.

4 · Policy live. The real closed loop, same start.

What each rung says

  • Rung 1, 20/20: the evaluation stack is sound; recordings reproduced bit for bit.
  • Rung 2, 0/20: a measured-state reference is not executable on this hand, even when perfect. Measured finger angles carry no squeeze (expert command leads measured by 0.25–0.6 rad in contact; force law adds ≤ 0.12 rad; bias −6.7 N). Part slips at the grasp in every task.

  • Rung 4, 0/20: live, the policy leaves its own demonstration within 10–20 frames, before any contact. Cause below.

Result 4Why the live rollout drifts: same first step, then the policy is shown different things

Rungs 3 and 4 use the same checkpoint from the same start and differ only in where the policy's inputs come from. Traced state by state on beam step 1, seed 1000 (no contact, zero force throughout).

Key point

  • State 0: identical observation in both runs → identical forecast (2×10−7 rad) → identical PD targets → identical simulator state through state 2 (7×10−9 rad). The policy is deterministic; no representation difference (deploy-side inputs and outputs equal the training-side computation to 0.0).
  • But that first move is not the expert's move. At reset every non-visual input is the same in every episode of a task (same robot pose, zero velocity, zero force, no contact), and the network puts ~zero weight on the point cloud: across 12 training episodes the state-0 arm forecast is identical (spread 0.000 rad) while the true first moves spread 0.065 rad (part spawn ±4–5 cm). It outputs the dataset-average first move. For this episode: cosine −0.34 with the expert's motion.
  • So after 2 frames both simulators are equally off the recording: 0.037 rad (thumb abduction), ~1–2 rad/s on hand joints, arm velocities of opposite sign.
  • State 2, the only difference between the runs: rung 3's policy is shown the recording's state 2 (as if nothing drifted); rung 4's policy is shown its own simulator's state 2.
  • That difference is amplified by the velocity input. Swap test at state 4, cloud and joint positions held fixed: recorded velocity → arm forecast cosine +0.84 with the expert; live velocity → −0.08. Swapping the joint positions instead changes it by 0.03. Second forecasts differ by 0.20 rad; from there the loop compounds.
  • Net: rung 3 looks normal only because it is told every 2 frames that it is where the expert was. Rung 4 is told the truth about its own robot and follows its own velocity instead of the scene.

Result 5Does the actuation channel matter? Position offset against feedforward torque

Every result so far generates fingertip force the same way: the law commands the joint past the contact and the position servo's own tracking error becomes the squeeze, dq = −K−1JTf. The standing objection is that position control is the wrong channel for closed-loop force control. So the force channel was given a second, opt-in route — a feedforward joint torque τ = −JTf added on top of the same servo — and the two were compared on identical episodes.

Experiment settings
Episodes
The 12 perfect expert episodes, 3 per task, each rollout a cold-start simulator process from the recorded initial state. 2 conditions × 12 = 24 rollouts, both arms run from the same working tree.
Reference
Oracle chunk policy: at every 12th frame the world is snapshotted, the expert policy is rolled 12 frames forward in the simulator to produce the reference states and forces, the snapshot is restored, and the chunk is executed through the middle layer. The reference is therefore generated online and the two arms share it only until their control diverges.
Premise
Measured (reached) joint states, so the law must supply the grasp preload — rung 2 of Result 3.
Position arm
Task-space law, defaults: kp 0.3, ki 10, per-joint offset clip 0.12 rad.
Torque arm
Same law and the same commanded force vector, delivered as joint torque instead, clipped at the servo effort limits (0.4 to 3.3 N·m). The position target keeps the reference plus the contact-point channel only, so the two never press at once.
Implementation
The physics engine's implicit PD accepts a feedforward effort on top of the position target, so no actuator model, gain or checkpoint changes; planning is always done with the torque switched off, in the dynamics the expert policy was trained in.

The two channels are the same actuator, up to their limit

With an implicit PD the joint torque is K(q* − q) + τff. The position arm adds K·dq = −JTf; the torque arm adds −JTf. The stiffness cancels, so the commanded torque is identical and only the saturation differs: a uniform 0.12 rad offset clip is a per-joint torque cap of 0.12·K, which is 0.11 N·m on the soft distal joints, against a servo limit up to 3.3 N·m. The channel is not the bottleneck here. Torque loses all three beam-step-1 episodes that the position offset wins, and that is the whole gap; elsewhere the two are close, and torque actually drops the part slightly less often. It also ends with the smallest force bias of any setting run so far: it tracks the force target best and does the task worst. That is what the mechanism predicts, since more available squeeze is exactly what a wider clip buys, and wider clips were already measured to be harmful in Weeks 1–2 (clip 0.20 rad: 0 of 12).

One place it helps. On screwing seed 0 the torque arm reached 6 of 10 sub-goals and held the part with a 9 mm closest approach, where the position arm reached 0 and dropped it. Screwing is the one task whose grip has to survive a long repeated re-grasp cycle, so more available force is plausibly the right trade there. It is a single episode and is treated as a lead, not a result.

Why the objection still stands off-simulator. The argument against position control for force loops is about stiff hardware servos, their bandwidth and the absence of a torque interface. This hand's simulated servos are soft enough (0.9 N·m/rad on the distal joints) that the position controller already behaves as a compliant force source, so switching channel only changes how hard the fingers may push. The limit remains the one Result 3 identified: a per-fingertip force law cannot reconstruct a grasp preload that the reference never contained.

Method note. The first version of this comparison was discarded. The feedforward effort buffer persists across simulator calls and is not cleared by the planner's snapshot and restore, so the expert policy was planning each chunk with the previous grip torque still applied; and the baseline it was compared against came from an older working tree. Both arms above were re-run after the fix, and the two conditions are now bit-identical until the first contact torque and diverge only at the next planning boundary.

Week 3

Sep 14 – Sep 20, 2026

Two results this week, both from the behaviour-cloning (BC) policy trained on the play2perfect assembly demonstrations. Result 1 measures how well the policy predicts fingertip contact and force offline, on held-out recordings. Result 2 runs the same policy closed loop in the simulator through the hybrid force controller, once tracking the predicted states only and once adding the predicted force targets through the force law.

The policy under test

A feed-forward behaviour-cloning network, one model for all four tasks. From one camera observation, the measured robot state and a task ID it predicts a chunk of 16 future measured states: joint positions, fingertip force vectors and contacts. It outputs references for the controller, not motor commands; it was never trained on expert actions.

Result 1Offline force and contact prediction

Given a recorded observation, the policy forecasts the next 267 ms: where every joint will be, which fingertips will be in contact, where on the fingertip, and with what force vector. The overlay video draws the forecast on the recorded camera frame it refers to, red for predicted contact and force, green for the recorded truth. The plot beside it shows the same episode as time series.

Seeds 20000 to 20004, identical initial states across the four conditions of each seed. With five episodes per cell, the 95 % interval on a 0/5 still reaches 43 %, so the grid cannot rank the conditions; it only shows that none of them works yet.

What the rollouts show. The part is never grasped. Within the first second the object height drops by 8 to 10 cm in every episode: the hand does not close fast enough, the part slips out, and the rest of the episode is spent near the table. Fingers do touch the part in half the episodes, and with the force law on the fingertips press harder on it (screwing above: 27 N against 5 N), but never hold it.

Where the error is. The controller follows the policy's reference closely: 6 mrad mean hand error, under 1 mrad on the arm, and the force law tracks its targets to within 1 N. The reference itself is wrong. From rest the policy under-predicts how fast the hand should close, and once forecasts build on the policy's own states the arm reference drifts 0.18 rad from the recording within 0.65 s. This is closed-loop covariate shift of the BC policy. The offline under-prediction of firm grasps in Result 1 points the same way: the force targets the law is asked to track are themselves too small.

Checks that passed. Replaying the recorded commands through the same live pipeline completes the tasks, and the live policy inputs match the dataset files bit for bit, so the wiring is not the cause. The velocity feedforward halves the arm tracking error but does not change the outcome.
